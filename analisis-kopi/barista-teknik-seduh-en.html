Brewing Starts with Clean, Ready Gear

Kompas.com ran a piece with expert pointers on how to brew better—useful precisely because good cups come from teachable, repeatable practice [1]. But recipes alone won’t save a shot; equipment hygiene and readiness matter. AEKI details the post-brew routine: discard the puck, rinse the portafilter and group head, clean the steam wand, then wash the drip tray and other removable parts [2].

AEKI also advises identifying the machine model and reading its care section first; only backflush if the model supports it, and handle descaling separately because it targets mineral scale, not coffee residues [2]. In short, consistent technique sits on the foundation of clean tools maintained per the manual, not assumptions.

SOPs Keep the Bar Predictable Across Shifts

Operationally, AEKI highlights opening–closing SOPs that name the task, the responsible person, the done criteria, and what to do when checks fail to meet the standard [5]. That way a team can decide when the bar is ready to accept orders and when the station is safe to leave [5].

The checklist also serves as a handover note so the next shift doesn’t rely on someone’s memory—useful for carrying unresolved issues forward and preventing repeat mistakes [5]. The goal is simple: consistency that doesn’t depend on a single person.

Hiring New Baristas: Honest, Specific Beats Grand Claims

AEKI’s interview guide says most questions probe three things: why you’re applying, proof of relevant skills, and how you behave in service work [3]. For beginners, honest, specific answers backed by small examples beat big, vague claims; don’t memorize rigidly because interviewers can change the order or dig for details [3].

On resumes, AEKI recommends one page that clearly shows relevant skills, how you learned them, and evidence. Café experience isn’t the only currency—training, measurable practice, student groups, and customer-service work also count. What to avoid: inflating skill levels just to pad the page [4]. That stance aligns with brewing and service goals: real competence, not just words.

Conclusion

These sources don’t promise magic—just solid basics: gear cleaned per the manual, SOPs that close gaps between shifts, and skills presented as they are. With those in place, dialing in recipes and service rhythm becomes much more manageable.
