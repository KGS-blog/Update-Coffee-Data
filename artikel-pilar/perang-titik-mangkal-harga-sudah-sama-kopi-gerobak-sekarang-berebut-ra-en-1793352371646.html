The Spot War: With Cart Coffee Prices Now the Same, the Battle Moves to Taste, Presence, and Trust

Cart coffee prices are now uniform, from Rp4,000 to Rp14,000, all at the same spot. Six business models fight in identical locations — and what is being contested is no longer price, but three other things.

October 30, 2026 · Also published on Kabar Kopi Blog

The Spot War: With Cart Coffee Prices Now the Same, the Battle Moves to Taste, Presence, and Trust Cart coffee prices are now uniform, from Rp4,000 to Rp14,000, all at the same spot. Six business models fight in identical locations — and what is being contested is no longer price, but three other things. By: Kabar Kopi Editorial | Ganjar Satyanagara · October 2026

Stand outside a station gate or an office tower, and you will see the same scene everywhere: colorful carts, a short queue, and customers done in two minutes. Nobody comes here to sit. What is being bought is a single thing: time.

At this point, one misconception needs clearing up first. Cafés sell space and atmosphere. Cart coffee sells presence — it stands exactly on the route customers walk every day. That fundamental difference determines who the real competitors are. Cart coffee does not fight cafés for lounge customers; it fights minimarkets, sachet coffee, and the other cart standing just down the street.

Why does everyone pile into the same spot? The reason is simple: that is where the people pass through. Jabodetabek's commuter rail recorded 349.31 million journeys through 2025 (IDX Channel). Transjakarta, MRT, and LRT reached 368.4 million passengers by September 2026 (Koran Jakarta). Cimigo's survey recorded 47 percent of respondents buying coffee from mobile vendors — a step behind minimarkets at 48 percent (Cimigo).

The problem: a busy spot for one player is usually busy for every player. Location becomes a contested resource, not a defensible advantage. In Kampung Starling, vendors carry an extra risk: Satpol PP raids that can halt sales at any time (LPDS).

Now, about price.

Traditional starling rose from Rp3,000 to Rp4,000–5,000 per glass in 2024, following a more than 70 percent surge in world Robusta prices (Metro TV). Modern mobile coffee sits in the Rp8,000–14,000 range (Fortune Indonesia). Jago starts at Rp8,000. The range is narrow, and that changes everything. When everyone sets nearly identical prices, price stops being a reason to choose. Cimigo does record price as important to 82 percent of consumers — but that is a reason to come to a cart, not a reason to pick this cart over the one next door.

The consumer's question changes too. No longer “whose coffee tastes best?” but “who is most certainly there?” Availability is the strongest factor in the same survey: 95 percent.

Six business models, six different answers

From this data, the report maps six player clusters. This is an analytical framework, not a survey finding, and I present it as such. Each answers a different consumer need — and each fights on its own battlefield.

Value-for-money answers the need for “café taste without the café price.” Its battlefield is taste consistency day after day — the same beans, measurements, and milk in every glass, explained clearly to the buyer.

Volume and FOMO answers the need for fast, cheap, and available right now. Traditional starling at Rp4,000–5,000 lives here, and its biggest failure is a single one: running out of stock at rush hour.

Franchise and partnership answers the need for the same taste at any location. Sekian Kopi claims more than 200 partners with a Rp56.9 million package; Jago itself admits that maintaining standards across hundreds of carts is its biggest challenge. Standardization is the battlefield — and a weakness the big players acknowledge themselves.

Brand extension brings brand trust to the sidewalk: Jiwa Group extends Janji Jiwa to hundreds of cart units, while several large brands choose not to join at all. What is contested here is trust — customers come because of the name, not the location.

App-based fleets turn availability from coincidence into scheduled service. Jago has a cart-tracking app (Fortune Indonesia), KSJ Locator tracks in real time within a 5 km radius (Selular.ID), and both serve corporate orders. Two big players have already invested here, but most small carts still rely on physical presence. One condition applies: reliable location data and production capacity — without it, the promise of “coffee arriving on time” breaks easily.

Traditional answers the oldest need: cheap coffee from someone you know. Vendors in Kwitang and surroundings survive through regular customers and WhatsApp orders from nearby offices (Detik) — not because people know their brand, but because a habit has been built over years (LPDS).

Customer relationships — for whom, and how

Back to the core question: if price can no longer be moved, what does “winning the customer” actually mean? Is it simply making people know your brand — awareness? Or sharpening positioning and added value?

The answer depends on the size of the player — and it is wrong to treat them the same.

For big players, the answer is indeed technology and scale: apps, loyalty programs, standardization across hundreds of carts, and enough capital to burn on awareness. The promise of “coffee coming to your point and time” can only be kept by players with location data, fleets, and capital. That is realistic — for them.

But MSMEs fight on the same ground with different weapons — and the data supports those weapons. Look at the traditional cluster: orders arrive via WhatsApp before office workers have a chance to come downstairs. That is not awareness. That is a relationship that has turned into a transaction habit.

At the small-cart level, customer relationships are not about being widely known — people who pass by every day already know the cart exists. What is fought for is three far simpler things, and they often fail: be remembered, be relied on, be asked for again. Remembered — regulars' orders memorized without asking. Relied on — the cart is there at the same time, every day, never moving around. Asked for again — there is a reason to come back tomorrow, whether stable taste, honest portions, or simply a greeting that never changes.

All three align with the data: availability 95 percent, practicality 77 percent, taste consistency 51 percent. And all three can be delivered without an app — just presence discipline and intentional consistency. Ironically, this is where big players have their self-acknowledged weakness: maintaining standards across hundreds of carts is far harder than maintaining one.

Risks rarely counted

Three risks rarely enter the calculation. First, saturation — the more players at the same spot, the thinner each cart's income; one Kelapa Gading case recorded a 50 percent decline, one case, not an average. Second, raids and permits. Third, the absence of official data: there is no verified cart count, companies have not published financial reports (Bisnis.com), and most revenue figures come from the companies' own statements (SWA).

Including one figure worth watching: KSJ's claimed 6 million glasses (Fortune Indonesia). Divided across three years and 1,500 riders, the daily average falls well below the per-partner claim. This number cannot be used for analysis without clarification.

Closing

Jabodetabek's cart coffee is entering a phase where location and price alone are not enough. But do not be misled: this is not a single-weapon war. The best-tasting coffee is hard to prove in a small cup drunk on the go — what can be fought for is consistency. Presence can be sold through apps or through the discipline of the same hours every day. And trust can come from a big brand or from a name memorized by regulars.

Big players win at awareness through apps and capital — that is their battlefield. But among three carts standing side by side, the customer walks to the one that knows them. Awareness may be big players' business; presence and trust can be built by anyone, including the smallest. That is why the spot war is not over — and will not end soon.
